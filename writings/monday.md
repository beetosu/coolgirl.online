title: The Week Starts on Monday
created-date: 2026-08-09

Our lives are filled with little bits of precedent that exist just by virtue of always existing. Sure, maybe they had some use for some archaic task hundreds of years ago, but that meaning had been divorced from them eons ago, and they now are just propped up by the overwhelming force of indifference. Usually this indifference is not unwarranted. It would be ridiculous to uproot everything in search of marginal improvements. Sometimes, though, we find ourselves in possession of a belief so obnoxiously disconnected from the average life and easy to uproot that it boggles the mind it hasn't been torn out. In my mind, that is Sunday's position as the "start" of the week.
## What "starts" a week?
Before we start assigning who out to kick off the week, I think it's important to establish what exactly it means to start a week. I understand there is historic reasoning as to why Sunday *was*, in the before times, but from my perspective this is a non-factor. I'm also sure that those meanings still hold some important value
to some, and while that's fine and dandy, I'm not going back to the Julian calendar because some people have some gripes with how the Gregorian calendar was laid out.

Indeed, in modern, secular life, the main thing diving days from each other is whether it is a day most people work or a day where they do not. Of course, not everyone works a 9-5, but those who don't inevitably feel the cosmic pull of the majority. The person who works night shifts isn't surprised that there aren't any 10am happy hours. Likewise, someone who works from Wednesday to Sunday isn't surprised how dead the Monday brunch scene is.

My assumption is that a secular Sunday proponent would argue that Sunday is the time in which we prepare for the weekdays, and thus acts like warm-up to the week proper. This feels ridiculous to me because it is a kind of prescriptive logic applied to basically nothing else. Is New Years Eve the start of the year because it's the time everyone makes their resolutions? Are warm-ups the start of a game because they're preparing to play?

Of course, we often define beginnings as when the action actually starts. You may dislike your 9-5, and think on those grounds the day you have to come back into work is a crummy way to start a week, but I would argue beginnings don't have to be good to be beginnings. If anything, having Sunday act as a weird cushion for the bad news of Monday only inspires more tension. It becomes the bomb under the table in a thriller movie as opposed to the afterglow of a hard fought week.  

We can play semantic games and say that a day being a "weekend" means it occupies one of the ends of the week, similar to bookends. But, I would argue this does not map to how people treat weekends. I have never heard Saturday/Sunday, as a pairing, be referred to as "weekends". Instead, they are collectively understood as "the weekend", not these opposite ends of 2 separate weeks, but a collective end to a week. If a holiday happens on a Monday or Friday, we say it's a "3-day weekend", not that one of our weekends was 2 days long.
## My real gripe
Unfortunately, I cannot act like this is some principled stance that I decided on purely logical grounds to dig my heels into. When I started taking working out much more seriously, a breaking point was when I realized my schedule was just too erratic to commit to working specific days of a week, every week. Instead, I decided that I would commit to working out 4 days a week, and fit it into my schedule on a week by week basis. More overhead, but more freedom to actually have a social life while committing to a routine.

The goal is always to keep my workouts during weekdays, freeing up my weekends for any full day commitments (or allowing me to not take *any* commitments). Early on, a problem arose: if I missed 2 week days, I *had* to work out on Saturday to fit into my "week". Sure I *could* have worked out the previous Sunday, but then I'd have to anticipate hiccups in my schedule, which is obviously easier said than done. In a moment of desperation, I had an epiphany: Why not use both weekends as the padding, so then if I was busy Saturday. I'd still have Sunday to fill in my obligations for the week? On the other end, should I have all of my workouts completed for the week, I'd be able to actually rest Sunday, instead of feeling this looming obligation to start my workout routine to "get ahead" of the week.
## Bringing it back to reality
I understand the above example is hyper-specific to my own rule-following tenancies, but it underpins my ultimate problem with treating the days of the weekend as two distinct entities. I can understand an old precedent when it serves some convenience. As much as people gripe about Fahrenheit, I see how it's more useful for more human applications like weather. The week starting on Sunday does not serve the average person.  It is a spectre of another time, in which we had strict definitions on what those days were for, and what we ought to be spending our time doing in those brief reprieves from the humdrum of modern work. Many of us believe we no longer adhere to those prescriptions, but yet they have carved into the stone the rivers we ride our lives in. Are these bends worth changing?
